    Section 7.3 Circle trigonometry

    Subsection Riding a Ferris wheel

    Taini is waiting in line to ride the Ferris Wheel. The bottom of the wheel is 6 feet high so that riders can get in the gondola (seats) and the spokes of the wheel are 47 feet long, which means at the center of the wheel is \(47+6=53\) feet high. A rider in the gondola is always 3 feet below the wheel.
    🔗
    Taini is nervous and has some questions. First, how high up will she be at the top? The top of the wheel is 47 feet above the center, so that is \(53 + 47 = 100\) feet high. The gondola hangs 3 feet below, so she will be \(100-3 = 97\) feet up. That sounds scary!
    🔗
    Taini knows that when the ride is over, they stop the wheel every 30° to let the riders get off and the new riders get on. She wonders how high she’ll be when she’s at each point. Let’s measure the angle in standard measure, meaning counterclockwise from the horizontal line through the center.
    🔗
    Taini is at exactly 0° when they first stop the wheel. At that angle, she is at 3 feet below the center height which is \(53-3= 50 \text{ feet}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    At the next stop of 30° we have a small right triangle with hypotenuse 47 feet (because it’s the spoke/radius).
    🔗
    The height, \(b\) feet, is the length of the side opposite to 30°, so using the definition of the trig ratios (SOH-CAH-TOA) we get the equation
    \begin{equation*} \sin(30^{\circ}) = \frac{\text{opposite to 30}^{\circ}}{\text{hypotenuse}}= \frac{b}{47} \end{equation*}
    Multiplying each side of the equation by 47 we get
    \begin{equation*} 47\sin(30^{\circ}) = \frac{b}{47}\times 47 = b \end{equation*}
    Rewriting the equation with the variable on the left we have
    \begin{equation*} b = 47\sin(30^{\circ}) = 23.5 \text{ feet} \end{equation*}
    Recall that \(b\) measures the height above the center, which is 53 feet, and the gondola sits 3 feet below the wheel. That means Taini will be
    \begin{equation*} 23.5 + 53 - 3 = 73.5 \text{ feet} \end{equation*}
    high at 30°.
    🔗
    By the way, were you surprised that \(47\sin(30^{\circ}) = 23.5\text{,}\) such a nice number? The reason is that 30° sits in one of our special triangles, the 30-60-90 triangle, and \(\sin(30^{\circ}) = \frac{1}{2}= 0.5\) exactly.
    🔗
    The variables are
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \text{ angle of Taini's gondola (degrees)} \sim \text{indep} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} H = \text{ height of Taini's gondola (feet)}\sim \text{dep} \end{equation*}
    Generalizing our calculation for 30° we see that the equation is
    \begin{equation*} H = 47 \sin(\theta) + 53 - 3 \end{equation*}
    which simplifies to
    \begin{equation*} H = 47 \sin(\theta) + 50 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For example, at the next stop of 60°, her height is
    \begin{equation*} H = 47 \sin(60^{\circ}) + 50 =47 \times \boxed{\texttt{sin}}(60)+50= 90.7031\ldots \approx 90.7 \text{ feet} \end{equation*}
    We also know that at 180° she is at \(53-3 = 50\) feet, and that at 270° she is back at \(6-3=3\) feet where she got on.
    🔗
    Let’s make a table of the values we know so far and use a calculator to find the other values.
    🔗
    \(\theta\) 0° 30° 60° 90° 120° 150° 180° 210° 240° 270°
    \(H\) 50 73.5 90.7 97 90.7 73.5 50 26.5 9.3 3
    🔗

    Subsection Circle definitions of the trigonometric ratios

    Wait a minute! How does our equation make sense for angles that are too big to fit into a right triangle? For example, how are \(\sin(90^{\circ})\text{,}\) \(\sin(120^{\circ})\text{,}\) \(\sin(150^{\circ})\text{,}\) \(\sin(180^{\circ})\text{,}\) … defined? These angles don’t fit in a right triangle!
    🔗
    When Taini gets home, she searches the Internet for information on trigonometric ratios. The website explains that sine, cosine, and tangent are defined for any angle, not just angles that fit into a right triangle (\(0^{\circ} < \theta < 90^{\circ}\)). The extended definition uses circles centered at the origin, the point \((0,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Taini draws a picture of a circle with radius 47 feet, the radius of the Ferris wheel. She draws an angle, labeled \(\theta\text{,}\) and the radius from the origin to the corresponding point \((x,y)\) on the circle.
    🔗
    What does this picture have to do with triangles? Taini cleverly draws a vertical line from the point on the unit circle to the \(x\)-axis to get a right triangle. The length of the horizontal leg along the \(x\)-axis equals the \(x\)-coordinate of the point on the circle, and the length of the vertical leg equals the \(y\)-coordinate of the point on the circle. Because the circle has radius 47, the hypotenuse of that triangle is 47.
    🔗
    Taini uses the right triangle definition of sine (SOH-CAH-TOA) to get
    \begin{equation*} \sin(\theta) = \frac{y}{47}\text{ and }\cos(\theta) = \frac{x}{47} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Aha! These equations can be used to define the sine and cosine for any angle. That is, for any angle \(\theta\text{,}\) find the point \((x,y)\) on a circle of radius \(r\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \sin(\theta) = \frac{y}{r}\text{ and }\cos(\theta) = \frac{x}{r} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can also use these equations to find the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates of the point on the circle if we know the radius \(r\) and angle \(\theta\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} x = r\cos(\theta) \text{ and }y = r\sin(\theta) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For practice, Taini draws a circle of radius 10 cm on her paper and adds in a line at 130°.
    🔗
    At the point \((x,y)\) where the line meets the circle, it looks like \(x \approx -6.5\text{ cm}\) and \(y \approx 7.5 \text{ cm}\text{.}\) Since the radius is \(r = 10 \text{ cm}\text{,}\) Taini estimates that
    \begin{equation*} \sin(130^{\circ}) \approx \frac{y}{r}= \frac{7.5}{10}=0.75 \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \cos(130^{\circ}) \approx \frac{x}{r}= \frac{-6.5}{10}=-0.65 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    When she checks on her calculator, she gets
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\texttt{sin}}(130)= 0.7660444431 \approx 0.77 \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{\texttt{cos}}(130)= -0.6427876097 \approx -0.64 \end{equation*}
    so her guesses were pretty close.
    🔗
