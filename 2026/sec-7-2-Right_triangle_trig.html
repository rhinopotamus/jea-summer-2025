    Section 7.2 Right triangle trigonometry

    Subsection Robotic arm

    Khemkhaeng works as a maintenance technician in a plant that assembles circuit boards for automobiles using robots. Specifically, a robotic arm on the assembly line picks up a circuit board from a horizontal feeder tray and inserts it into a car’s dashboard housing at a fixed angle.
    🔗
    There are safety sensors making sure the robotic arm doesn’t bump into other pieces of the car’s dashboard. To avoid the sensors, the arm extends along an angled path of 42 cm from its pivot position to the insertion slot. The insertion pathway rises at an angle of 28° relative to the horizontal feeder tray. How high above the feeder tray is the tip of the robotic arm when the circuit board is fully inserted (that means the robotic arm is touching the dashboard)?
    🔗
    To calculate the height, Khemkhaeng begins by drawing a picture and labeling the parts of the right triangle. How can Khemkhaeng find the height \(b\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Trigonometric ratios in a right triangle

    Enter trigonometry! There are three key trigonometric ratios that help us find the length of a side of a right triangle if we know the length of another side and the angles, like with Khemkhaeng’s robotic arm.
    🔗
    In a general right triangle, we label one angle as \(\theta\) (theta, prounounced “thay-tah”) and label the hypotenuse \(c\text{,}\) as usual. Normally it doesn’t matter which leg is labeled \(a\) and which is \(b\text{,}\) but in this picture we are going to label the side opposite to (across from) \(\theta\) as \(b\) and the side adjacent to (next to) \(\theta\) as \(a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The first trigonometric ratio is sine, abbreviated sin, is defined in a right triangle by
    \begin{equation*} \sin(\theta) = \frac{b}{c}= \frac{\text{opposite to } \theta}{\text{hypotenuse}} \end{equation*}
    The second ratio is cosine, abbreviated cos, is defined in a right triangle by
    \begin{equation*} \cos(\theta) = \frac{a}{c}= \frac{\text{adjacent to } \theta}{\text{hypotenuse}} \end{equation*}
    The third ratio is tangent, abbreviated tan, is defined in a right triangle by
    \begin{equation*} \tan(\theta) = \frac{b}{a}= \frac{\text{opposite to } \theta}{\text{adjacent to } \theta} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that \(\sin(\theta)\text{,}\) \(\cos(\theta)\text{,}\) and \(\tan(\theta)\) are prounounced “sine of theta”, “cosine of theta”, and “tangent of theta”.
    🔗
    Whatever unit we use to measure the lengths of the sides (like cm or feet or miles) will cancel in the fractions, meaning that the sine, cosine, and tangent of an angle are just numbers without units.
    🔗
    A quick side note on the values of trig ratios. First, since the legs of a right triangle are definitely shorter than the hypotenuse, each of the sine and cosine is a fraction where the top (numerator) is a smaller number than the bottom (denominator). So, the sine and cosine of any angle is always less than or equal to 1. On the other hand, tangent is the ratio of the lengths of the two legs. Since either leg can be longer, the tangent can be smaller than 1 or bigger than 1, or even equal to 1 if the legs happen to be the same length.
    🔗
    Any scientific calculator (or other computational software) can calculate the sine, cosine, and tangent of angles. You’ll need to make sure that your calculator knows you’ll be entering the angle in degrees. (There is another way to measure angles, called radians, that is used in calculus, but we’ll just use degrees in this textbook.) For example, check that you get
    \begin{equation*} \sin(28^{\circ}) = \boxed{\texttt{sin}}(28) = 0.4694715628\ldots \approx 0.4695 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There is a mnemonic (memory tool) that people use to remember all three of these formulas
    🔗
    SOH-CAH-TOA
    🔗
    🔗
    which is pronounced ``soh-kah-toh-uh’’. Notice that S = sine, C = cosine, T = tangent, O = opposite, A = adjacent, and H = hypotenuse. SOH-CAH-TOA doesn’t really mean anything, but if you repeat it enough, you’ll remember it.
    🔗
    Let’s use this information to answer Khemkhaeng’s question. We have
    \begin{equation*} \sin(28^{\circ}) = \frac{b}{42} \end{equation*}
    If we multiply both sides of the equation by 42 we get
    \begin{equation*} 42 \times \sin(28^{\circ}) = \frac{b}{42}\times 42 = b \end{equation*}
    Rewriting the equation so that \(b\) is on the left-hand side we get
    \begin{equation*} b = 42 \sin(28^{\circ}) = 42 \times \boxed{\texttt{sin}}(28) = 42 \times 0.4694715628\ldots = 19.717805637\ldots \approx 19.7 \text{ cm} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Resetting the robotic arm

    Khemkhaeng needs to reconfigure the feeder layout for a new design. The arm now needs to move 38 cm horizontally and 31 cm vertically to clear the safety sensors. At what angle (from the horizontal feeder tray) should he program the robotic arm? To begin, he draws a new picture.
    🔗
    Since Khemkhaeng knows the length of the side opposite to \(\theta\) and the length of the side adjacent to \(\theta\text{,}\) he can use the tangent ratio to write an equation involving \(\theta\text{.}\) He gets
    \begin{equation*} \tan(\theta) = \frac{\text{opposite to } \theta}{\text{adjacent to } \theta}= \frac{b}{a}= \frac{31}{38}= 0.8157894737\ldots \approx 0.8158 \end{equation*}
    How can Khemkhaeng find the angle \(\theta\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Inverse trigonometry in a right triangle

    Each trigonometric ratio defines a function where the independent variable is the angle (in degrees) and the dependent variable is the ratio (sine, cosine, or tangent of that angle). Luckily, each of these functions has an inverse function, a way to undo it. A way to go from the ratio back to the angle.
    🔗
    For example, Khemkhaeng wants to undo the tangent function, so he can use the inverse tangent function. One name of this function is arctangent, abbreviated arctan. On a calculator or computational software, the key sequence is often \(\boxed{\texttt{INV}}\boxed{\texttt{tan}}\text{.}\) Since Khemkhaeng knows
    \begin{equation*} \tan(\theta) = \frac{31}{38} \end{equation*}
    that means
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{31}{38}\right) = \boxed{\texttt{INV}}\boxed{\texttt{tan}}( 31 \div 38) = 39.207203505\ldots \approx 39.2^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The other two inverse trigonometric functions are arcsine, abbreviated arcsin with key sequence \(\boxed{\texttt{INV}}\boxed{\texttt{sin}}\text{,}\) and arccosine, abbreviated arccos with key sequence \(\boxed{\texttt{INV}}\boxed{\texttt{cos}}\text{.}\) Again, you will need to make sure your calculator (or software) is working with degrees.
    🔗
    In precalculus and calculus textbooks, these functions often go by strange looking names: \(\arcsin(x) = \sin^{-1}(x)\text{,}\) \(\arccos(x) = \cos^{-1}(x)\text{,}\) and \(\arctan(x) = \tan^{-1}(x)\text{.}\) In that context, instead of saying “arc sine”, “arc cosine”, and “arc tangent”, we say “inverse sine”, “inverse cosine”, and “inverse tangent”. The superscript -1 is not a power, it is a weird but standard way to denote an inverse function. We will stick to the arc names in this textbook.
    🔗
