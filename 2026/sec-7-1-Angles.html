    Section 7.1 Angles

    Subsection Neighborhood angles

    Tim is walking around his neighborhood in St. Paul, obsessing about angles. He sees angles, like, everywhere.
    🔗
    Tim notices the stately oak, the energetic maple, and the perfectly formed linden tree all have branches sticking out at funny angles – perhaps 45° for the linden, 60° for the maple, and up to 90° for the oak.
    🔗
    Tim compares the roof lines of the 1920s one-and-a-half story craftsman bungalows with their low-slung roofs at about 20° to the 1940s two-story tudors with their dramatic 40° pitched roofs.
    🔗
    He passes the local skate park and sees a few teens practicing their spins. A quick 180° frontside (where you end up facing the opposite direction), a 360° aerial (where you end up facing the same direction you started), and one kid even pulled off a 540° aerial!
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Angles in triangles

    Tim learned in geometry class that angles can be measured between any two lines. For example each pair of sides in a triangle has an angle between them. A fun fact is that the three angles in a triangle add up to 180°, the same number of degrees as a straight line.
    🔗
    For example, the triangular roof on the bungalow house has two angles of 20°, which means the larger angle is
    \begin{equation*} 180^{\circ}-20^{\circ}-20^{\circ}=140^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There’s a name for a triangle where two angles are equal. It’s called an isoceles triangle. The roof on the bungalow house (and the tudor house) form isoceles triangles. A fun fact about isoceles triangles is that the sides across from the matching angles have the same length. For example, the roof might have these dimensions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A special triangle

    The 60° of the maple tree branch sits within a right triangle. The missing angle is
    \begin{equation*} 180^{\circ}-90^{\circ}-60^{\circ} = 30^{\circ}. \end{equation*}
    Notice if we measure \(a=1\) foot up the tree and \(b\) feet out from the tree, it’s not immediately clear what \(b\) equals. We can label the length of the corresponding branch \(c\) feet.
    🔗
    Let’s figure out what \(b\) and \(c\) are. Look what happens if we put an upside-down copy of the triangle on top of our triangle.
    🔗
    Notice that the sides labeled \(b\) line up and the sides labeled \(a\) fall exactly in a straight line because
    \begin{equation*} 90^{\circ} + 90^{\circ} = 180^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    That entire side of the big outer triangle is length \(1+1=2\) feet.
    🔗
    The angles of the outer triangle are 60°, 60°, and \(30^{\circ} + 30^{\circ}=60^{\circ}\text{,}\) which means all three angles are equal. By symmetry, the lengths of the sides must all be equal too. Since the left-hand side has length 2 feet, we get \(c=2\) feet also.
    🔗
    By the way, when all three angles and all three sides of a triangle are equal, it is an equilateral triangle. So an isoceles triangles has two angles and two sides equal, but an equilateral triangle has three angles and three sides equal.
    🔗
    Let’s go back to our original triangle and use the Pythagorean Theorem to find the length \(b\text{.}\) We know
    \begin{equation*} a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}. \end{equation*}
    Evaluating at \(a=1\) and \(c=2\) we get
    \begin{equation*} 1^{2} + b^{2} = 2^{2} \end{equation*}
    That is,
    \begin{equation*} 1 + b^{2} = 4 \end{equation*}
    so subtracting 1 from each side we get \(b^{2} = 3\text{.}\) Therefore \(b = \sqrt{3}\approx 1.732 \text{ feet}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This is a special triangle because there were nice clean numbers for the lengths. In a precalculus or calculus class, you might see this triangle drawn in various positions.
    🔗
    In this 30-60-90 triangle, the hypotenuse is always length 2, the shortest side (of length 1) is across from (or “opposite”) to the smaller 30° angle, and the longer side opposite to the larger 60° angle is length \(\sqrt{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    You will see another special triangle, the isoceles right triangle (or 45-45-90 triangle, in the exercises.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Angles in standard position

    Not all angles fit in a right triangle or even any shape triangle. Like that 540° rotation of the skateboarder. A general way to measure angles is to start the measure on the positive horizontal axis, let’s label it the \(x\)-axis and rotate counterclockwise around. In case you’re not familiar with the hands on an analog clock, that means we head towards the positive vertical axis, which we’ll label the \(y\)-axis. For example, the picture shows measuring angles of 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°, and 180°.
    🔗
    Note that the straight angle, 180°, is halfway around the circle. A full rotation is 360°. That 540°? It’s one and half times around because
    \begin{equation*} 360^{\circ} + 180^{\circ} = 540^{\circ} \end{equation*}
    In our picture we also include three quarters of the way around which is \(3 \times 90^{\circ} = 270^{\circ}\) Note that 0° is just where we start.
    🔗
    We can even talk about negative angles if we imagine they rotate clockwise (the other direction) instead.
    🔗
    When we draw an angle starting on the positive \(x\)-axis and rotate counterclockwise for a positive angle and clockwise for a negative angle, we say the angle is drawn in standard position.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Naming angles with Greek letters

    Textbooks often name angles using Greek letters. Two commonly used Greek letters are
    \(\alpha\)
    🔗
    alpha (pronounced “al-fah”)
    🔗
    \(\beta\)
    🔗
    beta (pronounced “bay-tah”)
    🔗
    They are like the letters \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\) the first two letters of the alphabet. In fact, the word “alphabet” comes from the Greek language. So now you know.
    🔗
    Some other common Greek letter names for angles are
    \(\gamma\)
    🔗
    gamma (pronounced “gam-uh”)
    🔗
    \(\theta\)
    🔗
    theta (pronounced “thay-ta”)
    🔗
    \(\phi\)
    🔗
    phi (pronounced “fee” or “fie”)
    🔗
    🔗
